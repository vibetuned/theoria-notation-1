# The triad family

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. XVIII pp. 87–90. Original prose; the figures are the book's
own (`assets/book/fig65.musicxml`, `triads.musicxml`).

A **chord** is tones sounded together; the three-tone kind — root, third,
fifth — is the **triad**, and one grows on every degree of the key. Four
qualities cover them all: a major third with a minor third stacked above
makes **major**; reverse the pair for **minor**; two minor thirds make
**diminished**; two major thirds, **augmented**.

Three triads do most of music's work: **I, IV, and V**, the common
chords. The Roman numerals carry the quality on their face — the modern
convention sizes them (I major, ii minor, vii° diminished), sharpening
the book's practice. A triad may also stand on its third (first
inversion, the chord of the sixth) or its fifth (second inversion, the
six-four) — same tones, different bass, figured by the intervals above
it.

## Narration (video: TheTriadFamily)

1. Stack two thirds on every degree of the key: seven triads, four
   qualities among them.
2. Which quality depends only on the two thirds: major-then-minor is
   major; minor-then-major is minor; two minors, diminished; two majors,
   augmented.
3. The same construction works on the minor scale — the numerals wear
   their quality.
4. And of the seven, three carry most of the music you know: I, IV, V —
   the common chords, about to shake hands at the cadences.
