# The four cadences

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. XVIII pp. 90–94. Original prose; the figures are the book's
own (`assets/book/fig68-71.musicxml`).

A **cadence** is the close of a phrase — in harmony, its last two
chords. Four kinds cover the endings of nearly everything you will
play:

- **Authentic** — V to I, the most direct close there is. If the final
  chord carries its **root on top**, the cadence is *perfect*; a third
  or fifth on top makes it *imperfect*.
- **Plagal** — IV to I: the church close, the "Amen" of hymnody. The
  same perfect/imperfect rule applies.
- **Half-cadence** — the phrase comes to rest **on V**, arriving from
  the tonic chord: a comma rather than a period, and never the end of
  the piece.
- **Deceptive** — V promises the tonic and turns aside, most often to
  vi: the ending that isn't.

Around the cadences the chapter seeds the vocabulary the harmony courses
will grow: the dominant seventh, suspension and anticipation, sequence,
pedal point.

## Narration (video: TheFourCadences)

1. Every phrase must end somehow, and four endings cover nearly all of
   music. First the authentic: dominant to tonic, the full stop.
2. The plagal close: subdominant to tonic — the Amen your ear knows from
   every hymnal.
3. The half-cadence rests on the dominant: a comma, a held breath. It
   asks for another phrase.
4. And the deceptive cadence: the dominant promises home and steps to vi
   instead. Music's favorite trick.
5. One refinement: if the last chord has its root in the soprano, the
   close is perfect; anything else on top leaves it imperfect.
