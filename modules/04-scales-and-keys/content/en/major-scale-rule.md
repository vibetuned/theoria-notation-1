# The major scale rule

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. VII pp. 27–32. Original prose; the plate is the book's
fifteen-scale figure (`assets/book/scales.musicxml`).

A **scale** is a ladder of tones, all loyal to one home tone — the
**tonic**. Scales were not invented but discovered and systematized;
whole musics have been made from other ladders (a pentatonic tune needs
only five rungs). The **major** ladder has one defining pattern: **half
steps between degrees 3–4 and 7–8, whole steps everywhere else**.

Gehrkens' most elegant teaching tool is the **tetrachord**: split the
scale into two four-tone halves and they share the same shape
(whole–whole–half). Better still, the *upper* tetrachord of any sharp key
is the *lower* tetrachord of the next — the circle of keys is the same
four notes handed forward, fifteen times. Key and scale are the same tone
material; the scale is that material set out in order.

## Narration (video: TheMajorScaleRule)

1. A scale is a ladder — eight rungs, one home tone, and every other tone
   loyal to it.
2. What makes the ladder major is where the small steps fall: between
   three and four, and between seven and eight. Nowhere else.
3. Split the ladder in half and both halves match: whole, whole, half.
   And the top half of this key is the bottom half of the next — the
   fifteen keys are one pattern, passed hand to hand.
