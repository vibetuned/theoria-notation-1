# The three minor forms

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. VIII pp. 33–41. Original prose; the plate is the book's
A-minor figure (`assets/book/minor.musicxml`).

**Minor** begins with a smaller third — three half steps from tonic to
mediant instead of four. From there the scale wears three shapes:

- **Natural (original)** — exactly the key signature, nothing altered.
- **Harmonic** — the seventh raised, so the key has a true leading tone;
  the price is the step-and-a-half between 6 and 7, the one place a
  diatonic scale exceeds a whole step.
- **Melodic** — 6 and 7 both raised on the way up (smoothing the path to
  the tonic), both restored on the way down.

Every signature serves **two keys**: a major and its **relative minor**,
found three degrees below it (E minor shares G major's signature). The
raised seventh **never enters the signature** — it appears as an
accidental, every time. And the solfège shifts its anchor: minor sings
from LA.

## Narration (video: TheThreeMinors)

1. Shrink the third — three half steps instead of four — and the ladder
   turns minor.
2. One key, three shapes: natural, exactly as the signature says;
   harmonic, with its seventh raised into a leading tone; melodic,
   smoothing six and seven on the way up and restoring them coming down.
3. Look at the signature and you cannot tell major from minor — every
   signature serves both a major key and its relative minor, three
   degrees below.
4. One promise holds everywhere: the raised seventh is always written as
   an accidental, never in the signature.
