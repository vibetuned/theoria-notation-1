# Tetrachords: two matching halves

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. VII sec. 82, pp. 29–30 (figs. 53–55). Original prose.

Cut a major scale in the middle and you get two groups of four tones —
two **tetrachords** — that climb in exactly the same way: whole, whole,
half. A whole step joins them. This is the hidden machinery of the key
signatures: the upper tetrachord of C major is, unchanged, the lower
tetrachord of G major, whose new upper half needs F♯ to keep its
shape. The flat keys run the other way: the lower tetrachord of C
becomes the upper tetrachord of F major, whose new lower half needs B♭.

- same-steps: Each half climbs the same way: whole, whole, half.
- joined: A whole step joins the two halves.
- sharp-chain: The upper half of C major becomes the lower half of G major.
- f-sharp: Its new upper half needs F♯ to climb whole, whole, half.
- flat-chain: Flats run the other way: C major's lower half, an octave up, ends F major.
- b-flat: F major's new lower half needs B♭ for its half step.
- remember: Sharp keys: the upper tetrachord starts the next scale. Flat keys: the lower one ends it.
