# Rhythm, measure, tempo

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), chs. X–XII pp. 44–55. Original prose; the syncopation figure is
the book's own (`assets/book/fig56.musicxml`).

Gehrkens' pet cause is the word "time," which musicians abuse for three
different things. His repairs: **rhythm** is in the music itself — the
pattern of longs and shorts; **measure** is the accent-grouping we adopt
for it (say "three-quarter *measure*", never "three-four time");
**tempo** is the speed of the whole. Accent a normally weak beat and
rhythm audibly fights measure: **syncopation**.

Measures come **simple** (duple, triple) and **compound** (quadruple =
two duples, sextuple = two triples, on up), each with its accent
pattern. The **C** signature is a broken circle inherited from medieval
notation — not a letter. Tempo runs on the eight-family Italian ladder
from *grave* to *presto*, pinned exactly by the metronome, bent
**gradually** by *ritardando* and *accelerando* or **at once** by *meno
mosso* and *più mosso*, and restored by *a tempo*.

## Narration (video: RhythmVersusMeasure)

1. Three words hide inside "time." Rhythm: the music's own pattern of
   long and short. Measure: the grouping we count it in. Tempo: how fast
   the whole thing goes.
2. Here is rhythm quarrelling with measure — an accent where none
   belongs. Syncopation.
3. Duple and triple are the simple measures; the compounds stack them,
   and the accents mark the joints.
4. And that C at the clef? A broken circle, centuries old — not a letter
   at all.

## Narration (video: TheTempoWords)

1. Eight families of speed, from grave to presto — a ladder every score
   assumes you know.
2. The metronome pins the rungs precisely; the words bend them —
   gradually with ritardando and accelerando, instantly with meno mosso
   and più mosso.
3. Speak precisely and the three ideas stay apart: three-quarter
   measure, andante tempo, dotted rhythm. The word "time" can retire.
