# Sharps and flats: degree modifiers

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. II pp. 6–7, ch. III pp. 8–10. Original prose.

Gehrkens is emphatic about what a sharp *is*: it does not "raise a note"
— it makes a **staff degree represent the pitch a half step higher**.
The flat lowers what a degree means; doubles move a whole step; the
**natural** cancels either way. Six little rules (the book credits them
to Rice) cover every alteration: to raise a natural, sharp it; to raise
a sharp, double-sharp it; to raise a flat, natural it — and the mirror
three to lower.

Two more laws complete the grammar. An accidental governs **to the end
of its measure** — the barline cancels it, and only a tie carries it
across. And two spellings can share one sound: G♯ and A♭ are
**enharmonic** twins, different names for the same key of the piano.

## Narration (video: DegreeModifiers)

1. A sharp does not push a note anywhere. It changes what the staff
   degree MEANS — a half step higher.
2. Six rules cover every alteration: sharp a natural, double-sharp a
   sharp, natural a flat to raise — and their mirrors to lower.
3. Every accidental has a lifespan: to the barline and no further,
   unless a tie carries it over.
4. And two spellings can share one sound — G sharp and A flat, enharmonic
   twins. Spelling is grammar, not pitch.
