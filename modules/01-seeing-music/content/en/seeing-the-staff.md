# Seeing the staff

**Source**: Gehrkens, Music Notation and Terminology (A. S. Barnes,
1914), ch. II pp. 5–7. Original prose.

Written music is a map: **higher on the page is higher in pitch**. The
map's grid is the **staff** — five lines and the spaces between and
around them, every line and space a **degree**. But the grid is
anonymous until a **clef** signs it: the G clef curls around the line it
names G; the F clef's two dots embrace F. Change the clef and the same
degrees mean different tones — the clef *assigns* pitch, it doesn't
decorate.

Join the treble and bass staves and one short line floats between them:
**middle C**, the hinge of the **great staff**. Eleven lines, two clefs,
one hinge — every note in this course lives somewhere on that map, and
notes that overflow it ride on **leger lines**, private extensions of
the grid.

## Narration (video: SeeingTheStaff)

1. Written music is a map, and the rule of the map is simple: higher on
   the page, higher in pitch.
2. The grid is the staff — five lines, and every line and space a degree.
   The clef signs the grid: G curls around G, F embraces F.
3. Between the two staves, one small line: middle C, the hinge that
   joins them into the great staff.
4. Eleven lines, one hinge, two clefs — every note you will ever read
   lives here.

## Narration (video: HowNotesAreDrawn)

1. A note is a head, sometimes a stem, sometimes a hook — and each part
   knows its manners.
2. Below the middle line the stem points up from the right side; above,
   down from the left. On the line itself, either serves.
3. Short notes may share a beam instead of hooks; and the dot after a
   note always sits in a space. Small rules — but they are why pages of
   music look inevitable.
